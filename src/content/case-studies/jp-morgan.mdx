<TextBlock>
  Matrix is the system of record for technology assets in the firm. Setting up an application, a database, assigning people, software, that sort of thing. It also has Insights, and an AI assistant, so you can get ahead of a problem before it happens. Anyone in technology can use it. That is over 100,000 people: engineers, leaders, auditors, and the teams that run reports.
</TextBlock>

<TextBlock title="What it replaced">
  Before Matrix there was SEAL. It started life about twenty years ago, and it had been given sticky plaster treatment ever since. You could look up an application or create one. Nothing else. The scope never moved. Every two weeks the line-of-business admins who actually use the tool would go through what was wrong with it. There was a lot.
</TextBlock>

<TextBlock title="My role">
  I was the design lead. Any design went through me. I worked with a lead engineer for Matrix, one for AI, one for Insights, the architect, and the managing director.

  A lot of the plan was already done by the MD and the architect. We then had to make those ideas work. I would ask the MD, who was the person who could actually decide: what is this for, what does it fix, and who should I speak to. I would design the feature and bring it back. The engineers would usually find the gaps, and we would work out how to solve them. Not much of this was written down. Engineering was already using AI, and I used it to test ideas. The Figma file stayed the source of truth.
</TextBlock>

<TextBlock title="One product, not three">
  My view was that these tools had to become one experience. When I started, the teams were in silos. Insights on its own, the application journey on its own, AI on its own. Nobody had said they would need to come together in one platform, or a person would not actually use it, and it would not make sense.

  You are looking at an application. You want to know if it is resilient. You should be able to open Insights and see that application in real time. If that raises a question, you should be able to ask the AI to go further, without leaving.
</TextBlock>

<TextBlock title="Tiles, or a list">
  The MD and the architect had imagined viewing your applications in a modern way, as tiles. I was against it. There are 16 sections in an asset flow. The top task is viewing the application, and tiles would have people clicking around in different places for different information. I proposed we keep the side navigation from SEAL, with the sections listed. That is the quickest way to see the information, in order. People use this a lot. A pattern they already know might help.

  Teams can talk and get nowhere until there is something to look at. So I designed both. I ran a few tests. People said it is easier to find what they need when the sections are in front of them. The tiles meant scrolling, and they looked a bit messy and disorganised.

  The original idea also planned a lot of integrations, so manual entry would become obsolete. That has been a slow burner. Not all the services are integrated yet, which backed my version up. People still have to go through the asset themselves. The MD was hesitant. The results, and the way the work actually happens, were a strong argument. He made the call to keep the side navigation.
</TextBlock>

<TextBlock title="The sketch">
  Nobody had thought about how these pieces come together. I sketched an interface with a top navigation for the different areas, and search. The primary task is looking an asset up, so I treated search the way you would treat it in Google. I proposed the sketch at a stakeholder meeting, to get people aligned, because the product was not clearly defined. That sketch did it. Then we had a product.

  I designed the logo, the colours, and a design system we could use. Six months later it functions. You can view and create applications, check Insights on them, and ask the AI about them. Those were my ideas. Everyone else was thinking in technical language, and in back-end tasks. My job was to look at the problems people have today, fix them in the new product, and give them a better experience than SEAL.
</TextBlock>

<TextBlock title="Insights">
  People come to Insights the way a CIO does. They look at a line of business and ask what is on fire, what is not resilient, how much we are spending on compute, and what vulnerabilities are showing up in software that has not been upgraded to the latest patch. The filtering is extensive. You can look at your own estate, or at one of your teams.
</TextBlock>

<TextBlock title="The assistant">
  There is an ask, everywhere, to bake AI into a product. I started from what it is actually for here. Insights leads to questions. The usual path is to export the data to Excel and send it to someone else to interpret. I wanted you to look the information up in Matrix, see the data, and ask. Can I trust it. Is it accurate. It says this is resilient, but where in the world is it running, and what is it costing.

  You click AI assist and a drawer opens. The chat already knows what you are looking at, so you can start asking straight away. If you find a vulnerability, you can send it from the chat to someone else to action. It tracks the fix and tells you when it is done.

  We hit a problem for a few weeks. To use AI in the firm you need security clearance first. There was a serious amount of red tape before we could put it live, and we had to add a modal when you land on the page, telling you about the AI. Not ideal. It is one of those trade-offs you make to get it live.
</TextBlock>

<TextBlock title="What is live">
  The product is live in the bank, with up to 20,000 unique visits a day. It is a more complete product than SEAL ever was. You can search and create any asset, not just applications. You can see your estate in Insights, and query it with AI, in one platform. A lot of what used to be a manual back-end step in SEAL now happens in the product.

  The integrations are not all complete. When they are, they will fill fields from other services, so people type less in by hand, and less bad data gets entered.
</TextBlock>
